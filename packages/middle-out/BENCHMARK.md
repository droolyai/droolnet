## Layer 2 — compression of protocol and media metadata

`Ratio` is input bytes / output bytes. A codec that fails the byte-exact round-trip check
gets no ratio at all — the row reports the failure instead. The identity codec does no work,
so its throughput columns are left blank rather than filled with a meaningless number.

Two modes are measured for every corpus, because they answer different questions:

- `per-document` is the wire case: each envelope or manifest is compressed alone, with no
  cross-document context, exactly as it travels between peers.
- `single stream` is the archive case: the whole corpus concatenated into one buffer, which
  hands gzip and brotli a sliding window over every document at once.

middle-out is a *per-document* transcoder: it parses one canonical document into a typed middle
representation. A concatenation of 500 documents is not one document, so on the `single stream`
rows the transcoder declines, self-verification confirms the fallback, and the container stores
the input verbatim. That is what a ratio of exactly 1.0000 with a handful of extra bytes is: the
passthrough guarantee firing, visible as container overhead and nothing else.

## Weissman score

Standard: `gzip-9`. Challenger: `middle-out`.

```
W = alpha * (ratio / ratio_standard) * (log(time_standard) / log(time))
```

alpha = 1, set explicitly and never tuned.

Both times are compression times in us (microseconds) — the same unit for
challenger and standard — clamped below to 1 us,
so the logarithm stays finite and positive. The substituted formula is printed on every row,
so every value can be rechecked by hand.

Two properties of this formula are worth stating plainly before reading the column:

- **It is unit-dependent.** It takes `log` of a bare duration, so W changes if you switch to ms
  or seconds. These numbers are comparable only against other runs of this same harness.
- **It rewards speed, not only ratio.** A codec that compresses nothing can score above 1 by
  being fast, and a codec that compresses better but slower scores below it. The ratio column
  in the table above is the number that means something.

## What this does NOT prove

- **middle-out is not a pixel codec.** Nothing here competes with AV1, HEVC or H.264 at coding
  image data. Layer 1 stores encoded segments produced by those codecs exactly once; Layer 2
  compresses the envelopes and manifests around them. Neither re-encodes a frame.
- **The video segments are synthetic high-entropy bytes.** They stand in for real encoded
  video. That makes them the hard case for the compressors (there is nothing to model), but it
  also means the dedup ratios reflect the reuse pattern the generator was told to simulate —
  shared stingers, mirrored rungs, one re-upload — not a measured distribution from a real
  network. Point it at real segments to get a real number.
- **The `single stream` rows flatter the general-purpose compressors, on purpose.** Handed one
  concatenated stream, gzip and brotli can dedup within their own sliding window, and brotli
  at q11 does. That is the honest comparison and it is included. Layer 1 differs by being
  global and persistent: a chunk stored once is not re-sent to anyone, ever, across streams
  that no single compressor window will ever hold at the same time.
- **Timings are single-machine, single-run, one process.** Ratios are exact and portable; MB/s
  and therefore the Weissman score are not. Re-run on your own hardware.
- **The Weissman score is a made-up metric** from a television show, reported because it was
  asked for. alpha = 1 and the time unit is stated above; it is a presentation of the ratio
  and time numbers in the same table, not independent evidence.
